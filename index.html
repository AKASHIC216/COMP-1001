Module 1

Computer Fundamentals

Computers are part of nearly every aspect of modern life. We use them to communicate, complete schoolwork, conduct business, access information, entertain ourselves, and perform countless other tasks.

Although many people use computers every day, they may not know what is actually happening inside the computer. In this module, you will learn about the basic components of a computer and how hardware and software work together.

You do not need any previous technical experience for this module. The goal is not simply to memorize terminology. Instead, you will develop a practical understanding of how computers work and how the concepts discussed in this module relate to the technology you use every day.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Define a computer and describe its basic functions.
  • Describe the historical significance of ENIAC in the development of modern computing.
  • Distinguish between hardware and software.
  • Identify the major internal components of a computer and explain their purposes.
  • Explain the basic roles of the CPU, RAM, storage, motherboard, GPU, and power supply.
  • Distinguish between input, processing, output, and storage.
  • Compare common types of computers and computing devices.
  • Explain the difference between an operating system and application software.
  • Use basic computer specifications to describe and compare computers.

A Brief History of the Modern Computer

When we think about computers today, we might picture a laptop sitting on a desk, a powerful gaming PC, or even the smartphone in our pocket. Computers, however, have changed dramatically since the beginning of the modern computing era.

ENIAC

One of the most important early computers was the ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer). ENIAC is widely recognized as the first general-purpose electronic digital computer.

ENIAC was developed by John W. Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School of Electrical Engineering.

It was developed for the United States Army to help perform the enormous number of calculations required for artillery firing tables and ballistic trajectories.

How Big Was ENIAC?

Compared with the computers we use today, ENIAC was enormous.

  • It weighed approximately 30 tons.
  • It occupied approximately 1,800 square feet.
  • It contained more than 17,000 vacuum tubes.
  • It contained thousands of additional electronic components.
  • Its construction required millions of hand-soldered connections.

Despite its enormous size, ENIAC had only a fraction of the computing capabilities available in the devices we use today.

Improvements in electronic components eventually allowed computers to become dramatically smaller, faster, less expensive, and more accessible.

Did You Know?

Some of ENIAC's earliest programmers were women. The original programming team included Jean Bartik, Betty Holberton, Kathleen Antonelli, Marlyn Meltzer, Frances Spence, and Ruth Teitelbaum.

Their work helped develop early programming techniques at a time when programming a computer was very different from writing the software we use today.

Computers Are Everywhere

A computer no longer needs to weigh 30 tons or occupy an entire room. Modern computers come in an enormous variety of sizes, shapes, and designs.

Some computers are easy to recognize. You may have a laptop or desktop computer at home, use a gaming computer, or play games on an Xbox or PlayStation console.

Other computers are less obvious.

Computers can be found inside smartphones, smart televisions, Roku, Amazon Fire TV and Apple TV streaming devices, robotic vacuum cleaners such as a Roomba, automobiles, appliances, security systems, and countless other electronic devices.

Think About It

A modern streaming device may fit in the palm of your hand, while ENIAC occupied approximately 1,800 square feet.

Despite their enormous differences in size and capability, computers still depend on many of the same fundamental concepts: processing, memory, storage, input, output, hardware, and software.

Lesson 1.1 — What Is a Computer?

At its most basic level, a computer is an electronic device that accepts data, processes that data according to instructions, stores information, and produces output.

Example: Writing a Paper

Imagine that you are typing a paper using a word-processing program.

  • Input: You press keys on the keyboard.
  • Processing: The computer interprets those keystrokes.

Lesson 1.2 — Hardware and Software

Every modern computer system depends on both hardware and software. Understanding the difference between the two is one of the foundations of computer literacy.

Hardware

Hardware refers to the physical components of a computer. These are the parts that physically exist and can be touched.

Examples include:

  • Keyboard
  • Mouse
  • Monitor
  • CPU
  • RAM
  • SSD
  • Motherboard
  • Graphics card

Software

Software consists of programs and instructions that tell computer hardware what to do.

Examples include:

  • Microsoft Windows
  • Microsoft Word
  • Google Chrome
  • Mozilla Firefox
  • Games
  • Media players

Knowledge Check

  1. Microsoft Windows
  2. Google Chrome
  3. RAM
  4. Microsoft Word
Show Answer

C. RAM

RAM is a physical component installed inside a computer. The other choices are software.

Lesson 1.3 — Inside the Computer

Although computers can look very different from one another, modern computer systems share many common components.

Understanding what these components do will help you understand computer specifications, troubleshoot basic computer problems, and make informed decisions when purchasing or upgrading a computer.

Processor — Central Processing Unit (CPU)

The Central Processing Unit (CPU), commonly called the processor, executes instructions and performs many of the calculations required for a computer to operate.

CPUs are found in everything from desktop computers and servers to smartphones and embedded devices.

Several companies and processor families have played important roles in modern computing.

  • AMD (Advanced Micro Devices) — produces processor families such as AMD Ryzen.
  • Intel — has produced many generations of processors, including Pentium, Intel Core, and newer Intel Core Ultra processors.
  • Apple — has developed A-series processors for mobile devices and M-series processors for Mac computers and other devices.
  • Raspberry Pi — uses ARM-based systems-on-a-chip. For example, the Raspberry Pi 4 uses a Broadcom BCM2711 containing a quad-core 64-bit ARM Cortex-A72 CPU.

Embedded Computers

Not every computer looks like a traditional desktop or laptop. An embedded computer is a computer incorporated into another device to perform one or more specific functions.

Embedded computers can be found in devices such as:

  • Smartphones
  • Smart televisions
  • Streaming devices
  • Robotic vacuum cleaners
  • Automobiles
  • Home appliances
  • Security cameras
  • Internet of Things (IoT) devices

You may interact with embedded computers every day without thinking of them as computers.

Motherboard

The motherboard is the primary circuit board inside a computer.

It provides connections that allow the CPU, memory, storage devices, graphics hardware, and other components to communicate.

You can think of the motherboard as the central platform that brings the computer's major hardware components together.

Memory — Random Access Memory (RAM)

Random Access Memory (RAM) is temporary working memory used by the computer while programs are running.

When you open a web browser, edit a document, play a game, or run another application, information the computer currently needs can be placed into RAM so that it can be accessed quickly.

RAM is generally volatile, meaning that the information stored in RAM is lost when the computer loses power.

Storage

Storage allows a computer to retain the operating system, applications, documents, photographs, videos, and other information even after the computer is turned off.

Common storage technologies include:

  • HDD — Hard Disk Drive
  • SSD — Solid-State Drive
  • NVMe SSD — high-speed solid-state storage using the NVMe protocol
  • USB flash drive — portable storage that connects through USB

RAM vs. Storage — Think of a Desk and Filing Cabinet

Imagine that RAM is your desk. The larger your desk, the more books, papers, and other materials you can have open while you work.

Storage is your filing cabinet. It holds information that you want to keep even when you are no longer actively working with it.

Therefore, a computer advertised with 16 GB of RAM and a 1 TB SSD has 16 GB of working memory and approximately 1 TB of long-term storage.

GPU — Graphics Processing Unit

The Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) processes graphics and is designed to perform many calculations in parallel.

GPUs are commonly used for:

  • Displaying computer graphics
  • Gaming
  • Video editing
  • 3D rendering
  • Scientific computing
  • Artificial intelligence and machine learning

Some computers have graphics capabilities integrated into the processor, while others use a separate or discrete GPU.

PSU — Power Supply Unit

The Power Supply Unit (PSU) supplies electrical power to the components inside many desktop computers.

Different computing devices handle power differently. Desktop computers commonly contain an internal PSU, while laptops and smaller devices may use external power adapters and integrated power-management hardware.

Computer Case

The computer case physically houses and protects many of the internal components of a desktop computer.

Cases come in many different sizes and designs. Other types of computers, including laptops, tablets, smartphones, and embedded computers, package their components into much smaller enclosures.

Lesson 1.4 — Input and Output Devices

Computers need ways to receive information and ways to provide information back to users. These are known as input and output devices.

Input Devices

An input device sends information into a computer.

  • Keyboard
  • Mouse
  • Microphone
  • Camera or webcam
  • Scanner
  • Touchscreen
  • Game controller

Output Devices

An output device allows a computer to provide information to the user.

  • Monitor
  • Speakers
  • Headphones
  • Printer
  • Projector

Think About It

Is a touchscreen an input device or an output device?

Show Answer

A touchscreen is both.

The screen provides visual output to the user while touching the screen provides input to the computer.

Lesson 1.5 — Types of Computers

Computers come in many different forms. The appropriate type of computer depends on what the computer needs to accomplish.

Desktop Computer

A computer designed primarily for use at a desk or workstation.

Laptop

A portable computer containing an integrated display, keyboard, pointing device, and battery.

Tablet

A portable computer designed primarily around a touchscreen interface.

Smartphone

A mobile computing device combining communication, Internet access, applications, cameras, sensors, and general computing capabilities.

Workstation

A high-performance computer commonly designed for specialized professional or technical workloads.

Server

A computer designed to provide services, applications, data, or other resources to other computers.

Embedded Computer

A computer incorporated into another device to perform specific functions.

Single-Board Computer

A complete computer built primarily onto a single circuit board. The Raspberry Pi is a well-known example.

What About Virtual Computers?

Computers do not always correspond to one physical machine. Through a technology called virtualization, one physical computer can run multiple virtual computers called virtual machines.

Virtual machines can run their own operating systems and applications while sharing the physical resources of the host computer.

We will encounter concepts such as virtualization again as we explore operating systems, networking, and cybersecurity.

Operating Systems and Applications

Hardware alone does not make a modern computer useful. Computers also depend on software.

Operating Systems

One of the most important pieces of software is the operating system (OS).

The operating system manages the computer's hardware and provides an environment in which other software can operate.

Examples of operating systems and operating-system families include:

  • Microsoft Windows
  • macOS
  • Linux
  • Unix and Unix-like operating systems
  • Solaris
  • Raspberry Pi OS

Coming Up in Module 2

In the next module, you will work directly with Windows and Linux.

You will learn how each operating system organizes files and directories and practice navigating through those directories to locate, create, copy, move, and manage files.

Applications

Applications are software programs that allow users to perform particular tasks. Applications operate using services provided by the operating system.

Examples include:

Word Processing Microsoft Word, Apple Pages
Spreadsheets Microsoft Excel, Apple Numbers
Communication Microsoft Teams
Databases Microsoft Access
Web Browsers Firefox, Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, Brave, Opera, Safari
Media QuickTime Player

Module Summary

In this module, you explored the development of computers from the room-sized ENIAC to the wide variety of computing devices that surround us today.

You learned that despite enormous differences in size, appearance, and purpose, computers share several fundamental concepts.

  • Computers accept input.
  • Computers process information.
  • Computers produce output.
  • Computers store information.
  • Hardware consists of physical computer components.
  • Software provides instructions for computer hardware.
  • The operating system manages hardware and supports applications.

You also learned the basic purposes of the CPU, motherboard, RAM, storage, GPU, power supply, input devices, and output devices.

These concepts provide the foundation for the rest of COMP 1001 — Introduction to Computers.
